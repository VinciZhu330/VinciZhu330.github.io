## Preparation Phase

In early 2025, my older cousin passed away. I attended his farewell ceremony in March. As he was finally wheeled into the cremator, an indescribable feeling washed over me. I thought to myself: A vibrant life, lived for 30 years, has just come to an end. And one day, I too will leave this world. From that day on, I realized that life is simply a series of experiences. I wanted to enrich my own experiences and live a truly fulfilling life while I still could.
So, in May, I applied for my passport and visa in Shanghai. I did everything entirely on my own, as no one in my family had ever been abroad or held a passport before. Of course, I also sought advice from senior students who had been to Japan and relied heavily on online travel guides. Shanghai is China's most efficient city, so my passport and visa were processed quickly. As my final exams approached, I was constantly torn between my eager anticipation for the Japan trip and the anxiety of potentially failing my exams. Fortunately, I managed to push through the pressure, passed all my exams, and began planning my itinerary.
As for why I chose Japan: before he passed, my cousin introduced me to a lot of Japanese culture, like Samurai Warriors (Sengoku Basara) and the Nintendo Wii. Most of my beautiful childhood memories from winter and summer holidays back in my hometown were created with him. After he left, I decided to follow in his footsteps and see Japan for myself.
I only started booking flights, making itineraries, and asking my family for financial support a week before departure. Due to budget and commuting convenience, I ultimately decided to focus on four cities in the Kansai region.

## BE KOBE

On my first day, I landed at Kansai International Airport in Osaka. Right before landing, I borrowed a SIM ejector tool from a fellow passenger. As my phone suddenly caught a signal, I looked out the window and saw the Osaka skyline, which filled me with immense excitement—after all, this was my first time abroad, and I was completely alone.
After landing, I followed my plan to buy a ticket for the Kobe High-Speed Ferry. Unfortunately, I had to wait an hour. I went outside 30 minutes early to wait for the 4:15 PM connecting shuttle bus. That’s when I experienced my first heartwarming moment. A staff member, around 50 years old, approached me and gestured for me to go inside. Thinking I was waiting in the wrong spot, I was surprised when he used a translation app to tell me, "It's too hot outside, and the bus won't be here for a while. Please wait indoors."
![](接驳车站.jpg)
After taking the ferry to Kobe, I went to the restroom and missed the connecting shuttle to Kobe Airport. Seeing that the next bus wasn't for another 30 minutes, I felt completely lost. Then, an elderly man approached me and spoke in broken English, "To Kobe Airport, walk 5 minutes." This gave me hope and made me feel the warmth of the Japanese people all over again.
![](神户地铁站.jpg)
After a few transfers, I arrived at Meriken Park, a popular tourist spot in Kobe. I took a photo using the remote shutter on my Apple Watch in front of the "BE KOBE" sign. A few Japanese girls nearby were fascinated by this. They kept peeking at my phone screen and even mimicked my poses while I was taking the picture. It made me really happy; they seemed so friendly.
![](美利坚公园.jpg)
Then, I had my first meal in Japan: sushi, tempura, Kobe beef sukiyaki, and matcha ice cream. The restaurant was on the 18th floor of a mall, offering a stunning night view of Kobe. The servers were all grandmothers in their 60s who spoke English and provided impeccable service. When I finished paying and stepped into the elevator, one of the grandmothers bowed to me and didn't stand up straight until the elevator doors completely closed. I was deeply moved by Japan's highly advanced service culture.
After dinner, I visited an outdoor gear store downstairs. The clerk didn't speak much English, and even though the store was technically closed, he didn't turn me away. Instead, he showed me a rain umbrella and let me test it out.
![](神户夜景.jpg)
Finally, I reached my hotel. While most Japanese hotel staff speak English, their accents can sometimes be tricky to understand. In Japan, cash change, receipts, and room keys are always presented on a small tray. Not knowing this custom, I picked up the entire tray when the clerk placed my room card and receipt on it. The clerk quickly waved his hands saying, "No, no, no!" Realizing my mistake, I hurriedly put the tray back down. Haha, how embarrassing!
The hotel environment was great and had a bathtub. After walking all day, my clothes were soaked in sweat. I asked a convenience store clerk where the coin laundry was and experienced a self-service laundromat for the first time. You insert coins based on the load size, and it automatically starts washing. It also has a dryer, so you can wear your clothes right after.
I bought a "sleep-aid" drink at the convenience store but didn't drink it right away. That night, I was too excited to sleep. I ended up getting up at 1:30 AM to drink it, which finally helped me fall asleep.

## The Ancient Capital

I woke up to heavy rain on my second day. I bought a few sushi pieces from a convenience store for breakfast. You can't eat inside Japanese convenience stores, but you can dispose of your trash in their recycling bins outside. I stood huddled under the eaves of a building to eat, and the rain was so heavy my pants got completely soaked.
Later, I took a train to Kyoto. The sun came out as soon as I left the station. I visited Doshisha University. Japanese universities don't have walls. After a brief stroll around Doshisha, I took a bus to Kyoto University.
I first visited the Kyoto University Museum. Since I didn't know how to verify my student status and assumed Chinese university students couldn't get student tickets, I bought an adult ticket. For a university of its size, the museum was quite nice. It made me think about Chinese universities; we rarely see museums on campus, but we do build a lot of affiliated middle and primary schools, which seems to be a faster way to make money.
Leaving the museum, I explored Kyoto University. 
![](京都大学.jpg)
While taking photos of the library behind the main gate, I heard familiar Chinese voices. Two women were there; one was criticizing the other, who was older, for taking bad photos. Walking further in, I reached the Kyoto University cafeteria and met my first friend in Japan, Murakami. I approached him in English, but he didn't seem to understand, so we chatted using a translation app. He took me to a nearby restaurant for lunch, and we exchanged Instagram handles. Afterward, he showed me around the Research Institute for Mathematical Sciences and shared a lot of insights with me. We even took a photo together before I left. I am so grateful to have met him.
In the afternoon, I visited Nijo Castle, the former residence of Tokugawa Ieyasu. Most tourists there were white Westerners, along with some Chinese and Koreans. The highlight was the Ninomaru Palace. Photography is strictly prohibited inside, and you must take off your shoes. As the Japanese say, "No outdoor shoes allowed." The floors squeak like birds chirping when you step on them—a clever design by ancient craftsmen to warn of nighttime assassins. It was the first time I'd seen such an ingenious design. At Nijo Castle, I also saw a Chinese couple arguing. They were still at it when I left the castle. When will Chinese people learn to be more tolerant of their partners?
A small incident occurred as I was leaving Nijo Castle. I had just gotten a physical ICOCA card and wasn't sure how much balance was left. When getting off the bus, I didn't realize my balance was insufficient. The driver said, "Sumimasen" (Excuse me). I initially thought he was apologizing for the rainy weather. But the moment I stepped off, he yelled "SUMIMASEN!" in a loud, pirate-like roar. I realized I had essentially fare-dodged. I frantically fumbled with coins to pay the difference. So embarrassing!
![](二条城.jpg)
From Nijo Castle, I went to Honno-ji Temple, the site of Akechi Mitsuhide's betrayal of Oda Nobunaga. It has an exhibition hall dedicated to samurai swords and armor. I was amazed that swords from over a thousand years ago could remain in such pristine condition. Aside from a few from the 8th century, almost none showed rust or damage. This speaks volumes about the exquisite Japanese sword-forging techniques and the top-tier quality of the steel. A staff member kindly handed me a sword and took a commemorative photo for me. Japanese people are truly friendly.
Next to the exhibition was Honno-ji Temple itself. I was lucky enough to witness a prayer ceremony. The local monks invited me to join them. The process was: bow to the altar, bow to the Buddha statue, throw some incense-like spices into the burner, and finally bow to the chanting monks. 
![](本能寺.jpg)
Afterward, we sat down, and they began beating a drum. Every time I closed my eyes at the sound of the drum, it resonated deeply within my soul. I wonder if such ceremonies are still open to tourists for free in China? Probably not. Temples in China seem primarily profit-driven.
For dinner, I went to a McDonald's in Japan. An Indian girl who spoke excellent Japanese helped me order. The menu variety in Japan is roughly double that of China. I ordered a "Bai Big Mac," which has five meat patties. Interestingly, Japanese McDonald's fries are meant to be eaten plain, without ketchup. On my way to the laundromat, I got lost and asked for directions at a 7-Eleven. An Indian guy who understood English pointed me in the right way. I also bought four drinks there, and he asked at checkout, "Can you finish all these by yourself?" It seems Indian international students in Japan have also been deeply influenced by the local culture of frugality.
The nights in Kyoto are very quiet.

## The Origins of the Japanese People

On my third day, I headed to Nara. It poured rain again on the way, making Nara the "wettest" day of my entire trip. My pants were already soaked before I even arrived. Having skipped breakfast, I dragged my exhausted body to Nara and had a McDonald's breakfast. The Japanese McFlurry is smaller in portion but very sweet and rich in milk flavor.
Next was Nara Park. I had never seen so many deer in my life. They roamed the grass, wandered onto the streets outside the park, and even hid from the rain outside shops. These deer were nothing like the animals I imagined. I hadn't been to a zoo in ages, and I always assumed animals had to be leashed or caged. The Japanese, however, show them the utmost respect and tolerance, allowing them to coexist with residents and tourists.
![](鹿.jpg)
During interactions with the deer, Chinese tourists always seemed terrified of being bitten or charged. I heard a girl say in Chinese, panicking, "It's going to bite me!" while still hesitantly trying to feed it. What really made my blood boil (though I assume this was an isolated incident) was on the path to Todai-ji or some other shrine. A group of Chinese kids was provoking the deer. I could clearly hear what they were saying, yet their parents made no attempt to stop them. Enraged, the deer didn't hold back and started charging. The little boy dodged, and just as the deer was about to crash into me, it suddenly braked, realizing I was innocent. Even a deer understands this logic, yet Chinese parents who are educated enough to get a Japan visa fail to grasp it.
Coincidentally, the Chinese couple I saw at Nijo Castle yesterday was also in Nara today. It seemed their emotional rift had slightly healed. Cracks between people can always be mended by time; some take a night, while others might take a lifetime.
Walking along a mountain path, I reached Todai-ji Temple. I bought a ticket on-site and went straight in. The crowd density was far lower than any temple I've seen in China. I've been to Lingyin, Nanshan, and Hanshan temples; they feel less like places of worship and more like giant lottery companies selling "tickets" to Chinese people. Tourists make impossible wishes, hoping the "lottery company" will grant them in exchange for incense money.
Among the sparse crowd was a group of Chinese middle school students on a tour. Wearing matching yellow hats, they listened quietly to their guide without making noise or causing any annoyance. The younger generation in China is gradually aligning with higher standards of civic awareness.
Walking into the world's largest wooden structure, I saw the massive Vairocana Buddha. It was dark brown-black, very different from golden Chinese Buddhas. The black statue exuded a solemn, serene dignity, whereas golden ones make you feel like you've entered an opulent palace. This actually reflects the differing national characters. Chinese people tend to concentrate massive resources to make a single site extremely luxurious, sometimes crossing into extravagance. The Forbidden City and the Temple of Heaven in Beijing give me the same feeling, with vibrant colors, gold, and red emphasizing its status as the center of the world.
Japan, however, has long been on the periphery of the world. Before the Age of Discovery, it was arguably the civilization farthest from the true center of the world. Many things transmitted to Japan didn't travel further east. Thus, the Japanese have always seen themselves as the end of the world, cherishing these imported elements and hoping to develop them fully. This birthed various "Do" (ways/arts) and the "craftsmanship spirit" (since there is no next stop, you must make everything here as perfect as possible). Due to long-term resource scarcity, the Japanese are also frugal. They avoid overly ornate designs, favoring simplicity, pragmatism, humility, and understatement.
![](东大寺.jpg)
After Todai-ji, I took a bus back to my accommodation. For my night in Nara, I chose a self-check-in guesthouse, something I'd never tried before. Arriving at Haruya (the guesthouse name), I first found an envelope with my name in the mailbox by the door. Inside were the passwords for the main gate and my room. Stepping through the gate, I found a typical Japanese courtyard with a pebble path. The room had tatami mats, and traditional geta sandals were placed outside the door. I didn't see a single person for a while. The note in the envelope made me realize this was a completely unstaffed guesthouse. Payment was made by leaving cash on the bed, relying entirely on honesty. (Since I had paid online, I didn't need to pay anything upon arrival). This deeply made me realize that Japan is a trust-based society, where mutual trust is an unspoken norm.
For dinner, I decided to ignore recommendations from Xiaohongshu or social media and instead explore randomly, entering any place that caught my eye. The first was an izakaya. The landlady, sitting behind the bar, saw me peering through the glass and came out. Through a translator, I learned it required a reservation and was closed to walk-ins. I bowed and said goodbye, then moved on.
Eventually, I ended up at a Taiwanese noodle restaurant serving white chicken broth noodles. It was the thickest, saltiest chicken broth I've ever had. Beside me, a salaryman who had just gotten off work was slurping his ramen loudly. This is a Japanese dining custom; they believe eating ramen should be hearty and vigorous, unlike eating other foods. Besides the white chicken ramen, I also ordered the "Japanese carb trio": rice and gyoza. Honestly, halfway through, I regretted it. Too many carbs completely stuffed my stomach.

![](碳水三件套.jpg)
After dinner, night fell. I packed up and walked out into the quiet streets of Nara. Nara is always peaceful. Back at my lodging, I went straight to sleep.

## From Quiet to Lively
Nara is so quiet—almost excessively so—that I slept until 10 AM the next morning. I rushed to pack and head to the train station, with Osaka awaiting me. Passing through fields and traditional Japanese detached houses along the railway, I arrived at Osaka Umeda Station, then took the subway to Osaka University's Toyonaka Campus. Nestled on a hill, reaching the main campus requires walking up a mountain path. I noticed local students were well-adapted to the climb and walked much faster than I did. Along the way, I visited the university's history museum, the cafeteria, and saw the sumo club.
![](相扑部.jpg)
I also took a photo in front of the Mathematical Sciences building.
In the afternoon, I went to Expo '70 Commemorative Park, the site of Japan's 1970 World's Fair. Having bought tickets in advance, I entered smoothly. It's a massive park, and the first thing you see is the Tower of the Sun, designed by Taro Okamoto.
![](太阳之塔.jpg)
After taking a photo with it, I visited the Expo '70 Museum, which detailed how the Japanese organized the World's Fair, from project design to construction, covering every detail. At the exit, there were many vintage vending machines from that era selling instant noodles and cola.
![](自动贩卖机.jpg)
Walking through the park, I reached the National Museum of Ethnology. I communicated with the ticket seller in English and showed her my Chinese student ID. She sold me a student ticket. I wondered if the same thing happened in China, would a ticket seller accept a foreign student ID and easily sell a half-price ticket? The museum is divided by continent, showcasing various cultures with exquisite exhibits. 
![](民族博物馆.jpg)
Afterward, I went to Osaka University's Suita Campus for dinner. Entering the cafeteria, I faced a language barrier again and didn't know how to order. Luckily, a Black volunteer was there. International students usually speak English, and fortunately, foreigners can eat here too. You just follow the line, point at what you want, and no conversation is needed—perfect for introverts. I got a chicken cutlet with ketchup, mushroom and okra soup, pickled radish, and a bowl of rice for about 900 yen. It was incredibly satisfying.
![](大阪大学晚饭.jpg)
After checking into my hotel, I realized I wasn't far from Tsutenkaku in downtown Osaka. After a shower, I wandered around. I visited Don Quijote, which sells all sorts of goods, including an 18+ section with adult products. I didn't buy anything and left. 
![](唐吉诃德.jpg)
On the first floor was a pachinko parlor filled with an overwhelming number of machines. I'd never seen so many; perhaps gambling is banned in China. This made me realize I've always lived in an area with relatively limited material wealth. Japan has many products I'd only heard of but never seen in person.
Further ahead was the Shinsekai Hon-dori shopping district, bustling with various izakayas and lively energy. 
![](通天阁.jpg)
I even encountered Japanese bosozoku (biker gangs) riding motorcycles through the streets, with pedestrians yielding to them. Approaching Tsutenkaku, a police car with blaring sirens pulled up outside a convenience store, drawing a crowd. A Middle Eastern man was surrounded by police, apparently reported by a clerk for drinking inside.
I paused outside an izakaya, thought for a moment, and decided to go in. The staff immediately pointed to a sign at the register indicating a service charge. I nodded in agreement. I ordered a Kirin low-malt beer, sea bream sashimi, a sushi combo, and yakitori. The taste was pretty standard.
I always felt like I should buy something in Japan. Seeing the big "Tax-Free" sign outside a drugstore, a Chinese auntie working there spotted me and immediately said, "Hello!" Recognizing me as Chinese, I asked her for some pain-relief ointment and sleep-aid vitamins. A younger female clerk, who had the same Northeastern Chinese accent, chatted with the auntie in Chinese. While I understand the camaraderie, I felt this was unprofessional in a Japanese workplace. Imagine two Pakistani international students in a Chinese university discussing chemical engineering processes in their native language behind a professor's back; would that feel reassuring? At checkout, the younger Chinese clerk also tried to upsell me a rather useless vitamin bottle. Very typical Chinese retail behavior, haha. 

## Japan's Greatest Soldier
The next day was my last in Japan. After checking out, I grabbed a quick breakfast at a convenience store. I briefly visited Isshin-ji and Shitenno-ji temples before heading to Tennoji Zoo.
![](四天王寺.jpg)
Unfortunately, it was closed, so I went to the nearby Osaka City Museum of Fine Arts instead to see original Vincent van Gogh paintings. Photography was strictly prohibited, and I followed the rules, so I have no photos to show for it. In Japanese museums, people don't chaotically browse whatever they want. Instead, they form an orderly queue along the wall, viewing the paintings in sequence. The person in front finishes, and the next person moves up. This ensures everyone gets to see everything without causing congestion. The Japanese adherence to order made me reflect; sometimes, we all need a bit of order to make the world more efficient.
Taking a bus to Osaka Castle Park, I followed the crowd to the ticket gate.
![](大阪城.jpg)
Having bought my tickets on Klook in advance, I entered the main keep smoothly. Climbing the spiral stairs to the top, I got a panoramic view of Osaka. You suddenly realize that this is what a modern, clean, and decent city looks like.
![](大阪高处眺望.jpg)
A few floors down, I learned about Toyotomi Hideyoshi's life and the famous Battle of Osaka Summer Campaign, the final battle of Japan's Sengoku period. The legendary Sanada Yukimura died at Yasui Shrine after his defeat. After leaving Osaka Castle, I personally visited the shrine to pay my respects to Sanada Yukimura.
![](真田幸村.jpg)
Feeling a bit hungry, I took the subway to Shinsaibashi, Osaka's busiest commercial district. 
![](道顿掘.jpg)
The bustling streets and abundance of goods were unlike anything I'd ever seen in China. Unfortunately, my lunch was an eel rice bowl that tasted just okay. A large piece of eel was garnished with a halved egg. Probably due to high rent, this bowl of eel rice was extremely expensive. A buyer's shop named SELECTION caught my eye. Walking in, I was greeted by the GOATs of basketball and baseball: LeBron James and Shohei Ohtani. 
![](买手店.jpg)
The store was full of jerseys and sports merchandise. Ohtani's popularity in Japan undoubtedly surpasses that of any other athlete. One section was dedicated entirely to his merchandise: trading cards, signed photos, and signed baseballs. To the Japanese, he is not just an athlete but a hero (much like Japan's greatest soldier, Sanada Yukimura), holding a status similar to Yao Ming's in the hearts of the Chinese people.
![](大谷翔平周边.jpg)
I bought a pair of jeans and a denim shirt at Levi’s.
Checking my watch, I realized I had only three hours left before leaving Japan. I strolled through the streets of Osaka, hoping to build a stronger connection and create more memories with this city. I reached Nankai Station, a massive civic plaza where various political parties were campaigning, as my visit coincided with the election season. Staff members stood on trucks, using megaphones to promote their party's policies and ask for votes. A worker handed me a flyer for the Sanseito (Japan Innovation Party). After a quick glance, I found that this party opposes foreign immigration, focusing entirely on protecting the rights of native Japanese citizens. In a way, their stance is somewhat similar to Donald Trump's. Meanwhile, people from other parties were holding signs that read "I FIGHT RACISM," clearly showing their support for immigration. But all of this had nothing to do with me; it was their own domestic politics.
![](反对种族主义.jpg)
On the airport shuttle bus, I thought back to the confusion I felt when I first landed in Japan. Now, I was filled with reluctance to leave; I didn't want to go. But I had to return to China to finish my studies. I thought that maybe, upon graduation, I could come to Japan to study or work. The people here are full of kindness and a strong sense of boundaries. They won't bother you for no reason, but when you need help, they will put aside what they are doing and spare no effort to assist you. Sometimes I also wonder: aren't the ancient virtues they uphold the very same ones we believed in centuries ago? Years of war, foreign invasions, and ideological infiltration have shaped Chinese people into who we are today. Thinking about it sometimes brings a sense of regret; we were meant to be one of the great nations of this world. However, civic awareness in China is gradually improving today. The atomization of society has driven the younger generation to keep progressing, becoming increasingly respectful of public morality and social rules. I believe that in the future, China will only get better and better.
